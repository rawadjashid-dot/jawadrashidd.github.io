    You have to turn sideways to get through Nilkhet on a weekday afternoon. Books rise to the ceiling on both sides of the lane, engineering textbooks stacked against exam guides. You ask a shopkeeper for a title and he pulls it from a pile in under a minute, without a computer. A student opens a used copy and finds someone else’s notes in the margins, written before she was born.

    On paper, Nilkhet should be gone. A developer could put a shopping tower on that land and earn far more from it. You can order most of these books online and have them at your door within days, or find a PDF on your phone in seconds. A glass building with escalators would beat Nilkhet on any number a city planner cares about.

    Students keep coming anyway, as their parents did. They come for things no delivery app can give them. You pick up a book you were not looking for because its spine caught your eye. You bargain with a seller who remembers your face from last term. You hold two editions side by side and choose the one whose paper feels right. None of this is efficient, and students do it because they like it. I would call that taste.

    They also come because of what the place carries. Your teacher bought her books in these lanes, and so did your father. The shop you go to may have belonged to the same family for decades. A book from Nilkhet has the same words as one from a website, but you remember where you found it and who sold it to you. That memory is a kind of legacy, and a faster store cannot copy it.

    I think about Nilkhet whenever someone asks me whether artificial intelligence is going to replace us. AI looks a lot like the high-rise in this story. It is faster and cheaper than us at many tasks, and it never needs a tea break. A model can draft an email or write working code in less time than it takes you to find a seat on a Dhaka bus. On any spreadsheet that measures output per hour, it wins.

    Some things will go, the same way some customers left Nilkhet for online shops. If your job is mostly copying information from one place to another, a model will do it for a fraction of the cost, and pretending otherwise helps no one. Plenty of work in offices and banks looks like that.

    Games make the other half of the argument clear. AI tools can already turn a sentence into a small world you can walk around in, and one day a model may generate an entire open-world game over a weekend. Millions of players have spent thirteen years waiting for Grand Theft Auto VI anyway. Rockstar released the fifth game in 2013, promised the sixth for 2025, and pushed it back twice to November 2026. Fans complained about every delay, and almost none of them went looking for a substitute. They are waiting for the game made by the studio they trust, with the city and the characters those people chose. A thousand generated worlds would not change that.

    The same is true well outside gaming. A model can generate a thousand songs tonight, and you will still go to hear the singer whose voice you grew up with. Students can learn almost anything from a chatbot, and many of them will still remember one teacher who made a subject come alive. People value these things for who made them and what they meant, and output per hour has nothing to do with it.

    Taste also decides what gets made in the first place. A model predicts what people liked yesterday, because that is what its training data contains. Someone still has to decide what should exist tomorrow, put their name on it, and take the blame if it fails. A machine can help with the work, and it cannot want the result.

    I doubt we will see high-rises squash Nilkhet anytime soon. The towers will keep rising around it, and some of its customers will keep leaving for faster options. The lanes will stay full as long as people care where their books come from. I expect the same from AI. It will take over a large share of our routine work, and it will leave us the part we do because we care about it.
